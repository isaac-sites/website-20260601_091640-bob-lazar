## Where expertise stops and proof begins

The central credibility question is not whether Taylor understands aerospace systems. It is whether his expertise establishes the reality of extraordinary UAP claims.

The answer is no.

Technical qualifications can improve interpretation, but they do not replace evidence. A scientist may be highly qualified and still reach conclusions that other experts dispute. History contains many examples of accomplished researchers making incorrect judgements outside, or even within, their own specialities.

This distinction is especially important in UAP debates because much of the evidence remains inaccessible to independent review.

When Taylor discusses classified briefings, restricted datasets or government investigations, outside observers cannot fully evaluate the underlying material. The public is often asked to trust either his judgement or the judgement of institutions he references. That may justify taking claims seriously, but it is not the same as public verification.

A useful comparison is aviation accident investigation. An experienced aerospace engineer's opinion carries weight, but investigators normally still publish data, photographs, telemetry and reconstruction findings. The engineer's [credentials]({{ 'credentials/' | relative_url }}) increase confidence in the analysis; they do not substitute for the evidence itself.

The same principle applies to UAP claims.

The credibility issue here is not whether Taylor understands instrumentation. The issue is whether viewers receive enough information to reproduce or validate the conclusions.

For example, claims involving radiation spikes, signal interference, unusual aerial objects or equipment failures may be interesting. Yet unless the underlying measurements, calibration procedures, environmental controls and raw datasets are available, outside experts cannot fully assess them.

In that situation, technical authority can easily become mixed with evidential authority. The audience may conclude that a claim is stronger because a credentialled scientist made it, even when the supporting data remain limited.
